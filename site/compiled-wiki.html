02The problem

Retrieval has no memory of its own work

“Most people’s experience with LLMs and documents looks like RAG… This works, but the LLM is rediscovering knowledge from scratch on every question. There’s no accumulation.”Andrej Karpathy, llm-wiki gist, 4 April 2026
“The tedious part of maintaining a knowledge base is not the reading or the thinking — it’s the bookkeeping… Humans abandon wikis because the maintenance burden grows faster than the value.”Same gist. Remember this one; it comes back on slide 07

Ask the same question twice

A vector search answers from chunks, then throws the reasoning away. Ask again next month and the model re-derives the same synthesis, at the same cost, with no guarantee of the same answer.

The vault has the opposite problem

Our reference vault held 2,330 notes: daily logs, work entries, reading notes. The knowledge is in there, spread across notes written years apart, and nothing connects them.

The proposal

Compile once into pages that link to each other and cite their sources, then keep compiling as new material arrives. The artifact is persistent; the cross-references are already there.

03The pattern

Three layers, three operations

Karpathy’s gist is deliberately abstract, and credits Vannevar Bush’s Memex. The shape is simple enough to run by hand.

Layer 1 · immutable

Raw sources

Articles, papers, transcripts, or in our case the vault’s own notes. “The LLM reads from them but never modifies them.”

Layer 2 · agent-owned

The wiki

Entity, concept and comparison pages the agent writes and maintains, plus index.md as a catalog and log.md as an append-only record.

Layer 3 · yours

The schema

Conventions, frontmatter, tag taxonomy, thresholds. The contract that keeps the agent consistent across sessions.

The loop

Ingest · Query · Lint

Ingest a source (it “might touch 10-15 wiki pages”), answer questions from compiled pages and file good answers back, then lint for contradictions, stale claims and orphans.

“Obsidian is the IDE; the LLM is the programmer; the wiki is the codebase.”The analogy that makes the rest obvious: schema is the type system, lint is CI, log.md is the commit history

Reported to work “at moderate scale (~100 sources, ~hundreds of pages)” without embedding infrastructure. The widely repeated “400,000 words” figure traces to no primary source; don’t quote it.

What a real implementation has to pin down

What the measurements say

Microsoft’s GraphRAG work reports win rates of 72–83% on comprehensiveness and 62–82% on diversity against vector RAG, while vector RAG wins on directness; Microsoft also concedes indexing costs “that may be prohibitive”, which LazyGraphRAG addresses at a fraction of the cost. vendor-run

The one head-to-head for this pattern, a Tencent preprint, reports LLM-Wiki beating dense RAG and GraphRAG on multi-hop QA (HotpotQA 0.839 vs 0.764 and 0.771). industry preprint

State of the evidenceNothing in this literature is peer-reviewed, and the strongest claims come from the people selling the approach. Karpathy himself reports no measurement. Treat the pattern as promising practice, not settled science.

07The evidence that matters

This is the whole argument

The pattern’s promise is that the agent does the bookkeeping humans abandon. In practice the agent does the bookkeeping it happens to look at, and nobody runs the lint step because nothing forces them to.

Our own run says the same thing from the other side: when we asked the model to lint its own wiki, it looped for 120 tool calls and reported “all links resolve” while 13 were broken.

SoMake the mechanical checks deterministic and run them on a schedule or in CI, exactly like a linter on code. Keep the LLM for judgement: what deserves a page, what contradicts what, how to phrase it.
08Myth check

Three things not to repeat on stage

The most-cited numbers in this debate are misquoted in both directions.

“Studies show LLM-maintained context files degrade quality, while human-maintained ones improve it.”

The paper everyone means is Evaluating AGENTS.md (Feb 2026). It found context files “does not generally improve task success rates, while increasing inference cost by over 20% on average”, and that this holds for both LLM-generated and developer-committed files. It’s about coding-agent context files, not knowledge wikis. Both halves of the popular claim are wrong.

“Karpathy runs a wiki of ~100 articles and 400,000 words.”

Unsourced folklore, repeated across content farms. What the gist actually says is that it works “at moderate scale (~100 sources, ~hundreds of pages)”. Use that.

“The wiki pattern is 70× faster than RAG.”

Also unsourced, from a content farm. There is no benchmark behind it. The credible comparisons are the GraphRAG win rates and one industry preprint, both of which measure quality, not speed.

Half the writing about this pattern is recycled SEO content. Check anything before you put it on a slide; that includes this deck’s claims, which are sourced on the last slide.

09Limits

When a compiled wiki is the wrong tool

Infrastructure it doesn’t have

  • No access control: it’s a folder of Markdown
  • No transactions. Two agents writing the same page produce a race
  • No audit trail beyond log.md and git
  • RAG still wins for millions of documents, unpredictable queries, real-time data and multi-tenant deployments

Errors become permanent

  • A hallucinated connection is written down and cited by later pages
  • Practitioners report entries that are “confidently wrong” six months on, where nothing was reviewed
  • Rewrites flatten detail and homogenise voice over time
  • One academic trial found the wiki overstated its sources, presenting a workaround as established method

What separates the successes

  • A review gate: nothing gets promoted into the agent’s context unread
  • Thresholds, so the wiki stays small enough to review
  • Deterministic lint on a schedule
  • Git history, so every agent edit is a diff you can reject

A compiled wiki is a maintenance commitment, not a one-off import.

11Practice

Start with three notes, not three thousand

  1. Write the schema first. Domain, file naming, frontmatter, a 10–20 tag taxonomy, page thresholds. The agent obeys a written contract far better than an instruction in chat.
  2. Point it at your vault, not a copy. With an existing vault the notes are the immutable layer; pages cite them by wikilink and nothing gets duplicated.
  3. Compile one topic. Three short notes, at most five pages, each with at least two outbound links. Then read the pages: that’s where you learn what your schema forgot.
  4. Add a deterministic checker before the second compile. Dangling links, off-taxonomy tags, frontmatter, source links, orphan pages. Exit non-zero, and run it on a timer or in CI.
  5. Feed the checker’s output back to the agent, then re-run it. Ours converged in two rounds. Review the diff in git either way.
  6. Decide your review gate. Nothing enters the agent’s permanent context unread, or you get a knowledge base of confident nonsense in six months.
Cost, measuredCompiling three notes took 10 LLM calls at ~32K tokens each: about $0.20 on a mid-priced cloud model, or a few minutes of GPU time locally. The expensive part is your review, which is also the part that makes it work.
